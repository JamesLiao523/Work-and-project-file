UK pension provider, Aviva just made a standout move in the UK DC pension market: its default pension fund will put ~20–25% into private markets (PE, private credit, infra, real estate). That lines up with UK/EU policy pushing DC defaults toward long-term assets. 
The catch is DC defaults are usually daily-dealt, while these assets only allow at least monthly dealing for semi-liquid, meaning tighter cash buffers may need more liquidity monitoring.


Why DC plan invest in  liquid asset?
- Post payroll flows fast (daily/weekly contributions + employer match) and invest them promptly.
- Process member activity on demand: fund switches, transfers/rollovers in or out, ad-hoc withdrawals (US 401k loans/hardship, UK drawdown), and retirements.
- Maintain daily pricing/NAV for recordkeeping, statements, and comparisons under value-for-money rules.
- Rebalance frequently for target-date “glidepaths,” risk limits, and benchmark tracking without delay.


Public pensions (US examples): state & local government DB plans like CalPERS, CalSTRS, NYS Common, Texas TRS, Ohio PERS, etc. These are defined-benefit pools with long horizons and no daily-dealing promise to individuals, so they can hold lots of illiquid PE/VC/real assets.



IF a large employer change pension scheme(Master Trust) and switch out large% of the fund
- Uses cash buffer right away.
- Sells liquid stocks/bonds quickly.
- It cannot redeem the semi-liquid sleeve until its next window (and only after notice)
- The private-markets % jumps (because you sold liquid stuff), so remaining members are more concentrated in illiquids than intended—until you can rebalance later.

US (401k/DB): The employer’s Investment Committee (ERISA fiduciary) can hire/fire third-party managers or an OCIO to run bespoke mandates (e.g., LDI, target-date, white-label funds). Assets sit in a plan trust with a trustee/custodian; the recordkeeper handles admin.

UK (DC, master trust): The trustee board (not the employer) is the fiduciary that selects and oversees managers/funds. Employers usually plug into the scheme’s default/menu; you can influence or request a bespoke section, but the trustee/provider makes the call.

The European Commission is drafting reforms to expand ESMA’s supervision powers. If adopted, ESMA would take direct supervision of key cross-border players—stock exchanges, crypto firms, and clearing houses—reducing today’s patchwork of 27 national regulators and aiming for more consistent enforcement across the EU.
today, 27 national regulators do most of the day-to-day supervising, which can lead to uneven application. the commission’s new idea is to centralize some of that supervision at ESMA to reduce fragmentation and arbitrage
